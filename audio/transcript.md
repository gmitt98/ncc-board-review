This is a high yield audio review for the neurocritical care board examination, built from the second edition of The Practice of Neurocritical Care, published by the Neurocritical Care Society. It concentrates on the numbers, thresholds, first line choices, and trial results that are most likely to be tested. Each chapter of this recording is a topic, so you can skip to the areas you need. Numbers are spoken deliberately. Where the textbook gives a range, the range is stated. Let us begin with the physiology that underlies almost every question on the exam.

## Intracranial pressure and cerebral physiology

Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Normal cerebral perfusion pressure is 60 to 70 millimeters of mercury. The Brain Trauma Foundation target after severe traumatic brain injury is 60 to 70, and pushing perfusion pressure above 70 with fluids and vasopressors produced a five fold increase in acute respiratory distress syndrome without outcome benefit. The treatment threshold for intracranial pressure in traumatic brain injury is now 22 millimeters of mercury, derived from a retrospective study of 459 patients, while the American College of Surgeons uses 20 as an alert threshold.

Cerebral blood flow is governed by carbon dioxide. Cerebral arterioles begin to constrict when the arterial carbon dioxide tension falls below 35 and are maximally constricted below 20. They begin to dilate above 45 and are maximally dilated above 60. At a carbon dioxide tension of 20 to 25, cerebral blood flow is roughly halved. Strict normocapnia of 35 to 40 is the mainstay of intracranial pressure management. Hyperventilation is only a bridge for acute herniation, because renal compensation restores cerebrospinal fluid pH within about 4 hours, and because hyperventilated trauma patients had larger volumes of hypoperfused brain despite better looking pressure numbers.

The ischemic threshold for cerebral blood flow is 18 to 20 milliliters per 100 grams per minute in cortex. In subcortical white matter, where probes are placed, normal flow is about 20 and the ischemic threshold is 7.4.

Osmotherapy. Mannitol is dosed at 0.25 to 1.5 grams per kilogram as a bolus of 20 or 25 percent solution. Never run it as a continuous infusion. Track the osmolar gap, and hold the dose when the gap exceeds 20 milliosmoles per kilogram, which means incomplete clearance and risk of renal failure. Stop escalating osmolality at 320. Hypertonic saline can be given as 30 milliliters of 23.4 percent through a central line, or 3 percent at 2 to 4 milliliters per kilogram. Keep the sodium no higher than 155 to 160 and the chloride no higher than 110 to 115 to limit acute kidney injury. Hyperchloremia develops in about half of patients on continuous 3 percent saline, and the fix is a balanced solution of sodium chloride plus sodium acetate. Choose hypertonic saline as a plasma expander in the volume depleted patient and mannitol as a diuretic in the volume overloaded patient.

Tiered management of raised pressure: head of bed at 30 degrees, neutral neck, analgesia and sedation, then cerebrospinal fluid drainage, then osmotic therapy, then barbiturate coma or hypothermia as salvage. Steroids have no place in traumatic brain injury, ischemic stroke, or intracerebral hemorrhage. Dexamethasone 10 milligrams then 4 to 6 milligrams every 6 hours is only for edema around a neoplasm.

The Cushing triad is bradycardia, hypertension, and irregular respiration. Lundberg A waves, or plateau waves, are sustained pressure elevations recurring every 15 to 30 minutes once compliance is exhausted. On the intracranial pressure waveform, a compliant brain shows the percussion wave larger than the tidal wave larger than the dicrotic wave. When the tidal wave becomes the largest, compliance is lost.

## Airway and mechanical ventilation

Breathing patterns localize. Cheyne Stokes points to bilateral hemispheric or diencephalic injury, and also uremia, hepatic failure, and heart failure. Central neurogenic hyperventilation and apneustic breathing with 2 to 3 second inspiratory pauses point to the pons. Ataxic or Biot breathing localizes to the pontomedullary junction, and frank apnea to the ventrolateral medulla and the pre Botzinger complex. Kussmaul breathing means diabetic ketoacidosis or ethylene glycol.

More than two thirds of intubations in the neurocritical care unit are for airway protection. The five reasons to intubate are hypoxic failure, hypercarbic failure, airway protection with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 8 or less or bulbar dysfunction, expected deterioration, and a planned procedure. During intubation of a patient with intracranial hypertension, keep the mean arterial pressure at 80 to preserve perfusion pressure above 60, have the most experienced operator intubate, and return the head of bed to 30 degrees immediately afterward.

Induction agents. Etomidate at 0.3 milligrams per kilogram gives hemodynamic stability and lowers cerebral metabolic rate. Propofol at 1 to 2.5 milligrams per kilogram causes hypotension. Succinylcholine is contraindicated in myasthenia gravis, hyperkalemia risk, malignant hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, burns, denervation, and upper motor neuron injury, and must not be given between 3 days and 9 months after spinal cord injury. It raises potassium by 0.5 to 1 milliequivalent per liter and raises intracranial and intraocular pressure. Rocuronium at 1 milligram per kilogram is the alternative, but in myasthenia give half the usual dose, about 0.5 milligrams per kilogram.

Standard ventilator settings: tidal volume 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram of ideal body weight, plateau pressure below 30, positive end expiratory pressure of 5 to 8. Positive end expiratory pressure is safe in acute brain injury and does not meaningfully raise intracranial pressure. Lung protective ventilation in acute respiratory distress syndrome uses 6 milliliters per kilogram, but in the brain injured patient use a modified protocol to avoid hypercarbia. Severe neurologic injury is an absolute contraindication to extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. The Berlin definition grades acute respiratory distress syndrome by the ratio of arterial oxygen to inspired oxygen on at least 5 of positive end expiratory pressure: mild 201 to 300, moderate 101 to 200, severe 100 or less.

Spontaneous breathing trial readiness requires an intracranial pressure below 20, a Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale of 0 to minus 2, oxygen saturation above 90 percent on a fraction of inspired oxygen of 0.4 or less with positive end expiratory pressure of 8 or less, heart rate below 140, and mean arterial pressure above 60 on minimal pressors. Stop the trial for a rapid shallow breathing index above 105, a respiratory rate above 35 or below 7, a carbon dioxide above 50 or a rise of more than 15, a saturation below 90, or a heart rate above 140. In one prospective study, interrupted sedation had to be aborted in one third of comatose patients because of an intracranial pressure crisis.

Extubation failure occurs in 15 to 35 percent of primary brain injury and 30 to 40 percent of neuromuscular disease. Inability to follow commands alone should never preclude a trial of extubation. A Glasgow Coma Scale above 10, spontaneous eye opening, visual pursuit, and swallowing attempts predict success. Post extubation laryngeal edema causes 15 percent of reintubations and appears within 30 minutes in most; steroids given 4 hours before extubation prevent it in high risk patients. Percutaneous tracheostomy has about a 3 percent complication rate versus about 12 percent for surgical. Early tracheostomy in stroke did not improve outcome. In traumatic brain injury, early tracheostomy shortened ventilation and stay and reduced pneumonia but did not reduce mortality.

Ventilator associated pneumonia occurs more than 48 hours after intubation. Prevention is head of bed at 30 degrees or higher and chlorhexidine oral care. Empiric coverage of methicillin resistant staphylococcus, pseudomonas, and gram negatives for 7 days, narrowed to culture. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations, target a saturation of 88 to 92 percent and give prednisone 40 milligrams for 5 days. In severe asthma, albuterol every 20 minutes for three doses, methylprednisolone 40 to 80 milligrams per day, magnesium 2 grams, and heliox. Every ventilated patient with a pneumothorax needs a chest tube, because positive pressure maintains the leak. Needle decompression of tension pneumothorax in adults is at the fourth or fifth intercostal space in the midaxillary line, followed by chest tube. Neurogenic pulmonary edema is a catecholamine surge producing hydrostatic edema, and the treatment is the underlying neurologic problem plus supportive care.

## Acid base, sodium, and electrolytes

The anion gap is sodium minus chloride minus bicarbonate. The delta delta ratio is the rise in anion gap divided by the fall in bicarbonate. Normal is 1 to 2. Above 2 means a concurrent metabolic alkalosis. Below 1 means a concurrent normal gap hyperchloremic acidosis. Normal saline in large volume causes hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis by lowering the strong ion difference. Acetazolamide, topiramate, and zonisamide cause a normal gap acidosis by renal bicarbonate loss. Sodium bicarbonate has no role in lactic acidosis, does not improve pressor response, and has no effect on 28 day mortality. Severe alkalemia is a pH above 7.6, causing hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, tetany, and arrhythmias. Chloride responsive alkalosis has a urine chloride below 15 and responds to volume and chloride. In hypervolemic patients use acetazolamide.

Hyponatremia. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone is a diagnosis of exclusion: plasma osmolarity below 270, urine osmolarity above 100, euvolemia, urine sodium above 20, and no diuretics or endocrine failure. Cerebral salt wasting shares every laboratory feature but the patient is hypovolemic. Because volume status is unreliable at the bedside, in acute intracranial disease use hypertonic saline rather than fluid restriction or vasopressin antagonists. In subarachnoid hemorrhage, never restrict fluid, because volume contraction raises the risk of delayed cerebral ischemia; use normal saline, then 1.5 to 3 percent saline, and add fludrocortisone 0.2 to 0.4 milligrams a day for salt wasting. Severe symptomatic hyponatremia, sodium below 120 with seizures or stupor, gets 3 percent saline in boluses of 150 to 250 milliliters. Limit correction to 8 to 10 milliequivalents in the first 24 hours and 18 in 48 hours. In high risk patients, meaning sodium at or below 105, hypokalemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, or liver disease, do not exceed 8 in 24 hours.

Central diabetes insipidus: sodium above 145, serum osmolality above 295, urine osmolality below 200 or 300 depending on the chapter, specific gravity below 1.005, and urine output above 200 to 300 milliliters per hour for 2 consecutive hours. It signals impending brain death in most subarachnoid hemorrhage and trauma patients, and it is the most common immediate complication of transsphenoidal surgery at about 7.6 percent. Delayed hyponatremia from inappropriate antidiuresis follows in 4 to 10 percent at 7 to 10 days. The triphasic response is diabetes insipidus, then antidiuresis, then permanent diabetes insipidus. Desmopressin is a pure V2 agonist, 1 to 2 micrograms, onset 1 to 2 hours, duration 6 to 12 hours, for the stable patient. Vasopressin is V1 and V2, onset 5 to 20 minutes, duration 10 to 20 minutes, and is the titratable infusion for the unstable patient. Correct hypernatremia at no more than 0.5 milliequivalents per hour, and in a patient with resolving cerebral edema, no more than 2 to 4 milliequivalents per day to prevent rebound edema.

Magnesium. Hypomagnesemia below 1.5 must be suspected in refractory hypokalemia and unexplained hypocalcemia. Replace with 2 to 6 grams no faster than 1 gram per hour, keeping the level above 2. Hypermagnesemia causes loss of reflexes above 10, respiratory arrest above 15, and cardiac arrest at 20 to 25; dialysis is the treatment. The FAST MAG trial showed magnesium is safe but ineffective in stroke, IMASH and MASH 2 were negative in subarachnoid hemorrhage, and the one proven neurologic indication is eclamptic seizures, where magnesium beats both diazepam and phenytoin.

## Cardiovascular monitoring

Arterial oxygen content is 1.34 times hemoglobin times saturation plus 0.0031 times the arterial oxygen tension. An oscillometric cuff computes mean pressure as diastolic plus one third of pulse pressure, which fails at fast heart rates. The arterial transducer is leveled at the phlebostatic axis, fourth intercostal space at the midaxillary line. An underdamped waveform is narrow and peaked and overestimates systolic pressure; it comes from stiff tubing and is seen in one third of patients. An overdamped waveform is rounded and underestimates systolic pressure; it comes from air, clot, kinks, or low flush bag pressure. Mean pressure is least affected by damping.

Central venous pressure correlates poorly with volume, and any value from 6 to 20 may be normovolemic. Pulse pressure variation above 10 percent and stroke volume variation of 15 percent or more predict fluid responsiveness. In a spontaneously breathing patient, an inferior vena cava of 2.1 centimeters or less that collapses more than 50 percent suggests a right atrial pressure of 0 to 5 and likely fluid responsiveness. In a ventilated patient use distensibility, and an index above 18 percent predicts responsiveness. Normal pulmonary artery occlusion pressure is 6 to 12, read at end expiration. The most common pulmonary artery catheter complication is ventricular arrhythmia, and trials showed the catheter conferred no benefit.

The corrected QT is normal below 0.45 seconds in men and 0.46 in women. Pulse oximetry is only accurate above 75 percent saturation and above a hemoglobin of 5, and it cannot see methemoglobin or carboxyhemoglobin. A colorimetric detector turning from purple to yellow confirms tracheal placement. During resuscitation, an end tidal carbon dioxide above 10 indicates adequate compressions, and an abrupt rise means return of circulation.

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy after subarachnoid hemorrhage: QT prolongation, inverted precordial T waves, troponin rise, apical ballooning, normal coronaries, and beta blockade has some support. In cocaine associated coronary syndrome, avoid beta blockers and use benzodiazepines and calcium channel blockers. The percutaneous intervention window is 90 minutes at a capable hospital and 120 minutes if transferred.

## Glucose, endocrine, and nutrition

Glucose targets. After ischemic stroke, 140 to 180 milligrams per deciliter. In subarachnoid hemorrhage, above 80 and below 200. In intracerebral hemorrhage, 80 to 180. The NICE SUGAR trial showed intensive control to 81 to 108 increased 90 day mortality by 2.6 percent absolute with severe hypoglycemia in 6.8 versus 0.5 percent. The SHINE trial in 1151 stroke patients found no benefit of insulin infusion targeting 80 to 130 over subcutaneous insulin.

Diabetic ketoacidosis: glucose above 250, pH below 7.30, bicarbonate below 18, anion gap above 10, ketones. Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome: glucose above 600, pH above 7.30, bicarbonate above 18, osmolality above 320, with higher mortality and frequent coma. Give 15 to 20 milliliters per kilogram of isotonic saline in the first hour. If potassium is below 3.3, hold insulin and give potassium chloride at 20 milliequivalents per hour first. Insulin is 0.1 units per kilogram bolus then 0.1 per kilogram per hour, halved with dextrose added when glucose reaches 250 to 300. Lower glucose no faster than 100 per hour to avoid cerebral edema, which is more common under age 40. Bicarbonate only for a pH below 6.9. For hypoglycemia, 25 grams of 50 percent dextrose, or glucagon 1 milligram without access, and give thiamine before dextrose in any comatose alcoholic or malnourished patient.

Myxedema coma: altered mentation, hypothermia, and a precipitant. Give hydrocortisone 50 to 100 milligrams every 8 hours before intravenous levothyroxine, because thyroid hormone increases cortisol metabolism and precipitates adrenal crisis. Pituitary apoplexy with adrenal crisis gets hydrocortisone 100 to 500 milligrams then 50 to 100 every 6 hours.

Nutrition. Start enteral feeding within 24 to 48 hours. Energy is 25 to 30 kilocalories per kilogram of actual weight when body mass index is under 30, 11 to 14 per kilogram of actual weight for body mass index 30 to 50, and 22 to 25 per kilogram of ideal weight above 50. Protein is 1.2 to 2 grams per kilogram, up to 2.5 on renal replacement, and 1.5 to 2.5 in traumatic brain injury. Albumin, prealbumin, and transferrin are no longer used for nutritional assessment; use nitrogen balance from a 24 hour urine urea nitrogen. Propofol delivers 1.1 kilocalories per milliliter and clevidipine 2 per milliliter, so subtract those and check triglycerides every 3 days. Gastric residual checks are no longer recommended, and do not hold feeds for a residual under 500. Phenytoin binds enteral formula, so hold feeds 1 to 2 hours before and after, and the same applies to fluoroquinolones. The FOOD trial: routine supplements did not help, early tube feeding may improve survival in dysphagic stroke, and nasogastric feeding beat gastrostomy in the first 2 to 3 weeks. In trauma, deliver 25 to 30 kilocalories and 1 to 2 grams of protein per kilogram. In traumatic brain injury, reach full calories by day 5 and no later than day 7, and feed postpylorically to reduce pneumonia.

## Trauma and burns

Damage control resuscitation minimizes crystalloid and gives red cells, plasma, and platelets in a 1 to 1 to 1 ratio, which reduced exsanguination deaths at 24 hours without changing overall mortality. Tranexamic acid must be given within 3 hours of injury. Thoracotomy for hemothorax is indicated for more than 1500 milliliters of initial drainage or more than 200 per hour for 4 hours. An open book pelvic fracture gets a binder or a bedsheet. Abdominal compartment syndrome is sustained bladder pressure above 20 with new organ dysfunction, treated with decompressive laparotomy. In burns, a compartment pressure above 30 gets escharotomy first. Prophylactic inferior vena cava filters are not recommended.

Rule of nines: 9 percent for the head, 4.5 for each arm, 9 for each leg, 18 for the front of the torso, 18 for the back, 1 for genitalia. Parkland is 4 milliliters per kilogram per percent burned, modified Brooke is 2, half in the first 8 hours, titrated to urine output of 0.5 milliliters per kilogram per hour in adults and 1 in children. Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with 200 times the affinity of oxygen. Symptoms appear at 20 to 30 percent carboxyhemoglobin, above 40 is severe, and death usually occurs at 80 or higher. The half life is 4 to 5 hours on room air, about half that on 100 percent oxygen, and under 30 minutes at 2.5 atmospheres. Ten to 30 percent of survivors develop delayed neurologic sequelae 3 to 240 days later. Severe hypothermia is rewarmed actively, and hypothermia below 32 degrees prolongs the PR and QT intervals.

## Acute ischemic stroke

Alteplase is 0.9 milligrams per kilogram to a maximum of 90 milligrams, 10 percent as a bolus over 1 minute and the rest over 1 hour, up to 4.5 hours from onset by guideline even though the label says 3. Laboratory exclusions: glucose above 400 or below 50, international normalized ratio above 1.7, and current direct thrombin or factor Xa inhibitor. Other exclusions: major surgery within 14 days, gastrointestinal bleeding within 21 days, stroke or severe head trauma within 3 months, and blood pressure above 185 over 110. Bring pressure below 185 over 110 before the drug and keep it below 180 over 105 for 24 hours, using labetalol 10 to 20 milligrams, nicardipine starting at 5 milligrams per hour to a maximum of 15, or clevidipine 1 to 2 doubled every 2 to 5 minutes to a maximum of 21. Treating a stroke mimic with alteplase is safe, and mimics are up to 20 percent of treated cases. No antithrombotics for 24 hours after thrombolysis. Orolingual angioedema: stop alteplase, hold angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, methylprednisolone 125, diphenhydramine 50, famotidine 20, then epinephrine if it progresses. Symptomatic hemorrhage after alteplase: cryoprecipitate 10 units, more if fibrinogen is under 150, or tranexamic acid 10 to 15 milligrams per kilogram.

Extended windows. WAKE UP used diffusion positive, FLAIR negative mismatch for unknown onset: favorable outcome 53 versus 42 percent. EXTEND supported perfusion selected thrombolysis at 4.5 to 9 hours. Tenecteplase is 0.25 milligrams per kilogram to a maximum of 25 as a single bolus, noninferior to alteplase, with reperfusion in 22 versus 10 percent, and guidelines allow it in thrombectomy eligible patients.

Thrombectomy. Large vessel occlusion means carotid terminus, M1, dominant M2, or basilar. The pooled analysis of MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, REVASCAT, SWIFT PRIME, and EXTEND IA in 1287 patients showed benefit within 6 hours with a number needed to treat of 2 to 3, and waiting to see if alteplase works is explicitly not recommended. DAWN enrolled 6 to 24 hours with clinical core mismatch: group A is age 80 or older, stroke scale 10 or more, core under 21 milliliters; group B is under 80, scale 10 or more, core under 31; group C is under 80, scale above 20, core 31 to 50. Functional independence was 49 versus 13 percent. DEFUSE 3 enrolled 6 to 16 hours with core under 70 milliliters and a mismatch ratio above 1.8, with mortality falling from 26 to 14 percent. Anesthesia choice is genuine equipoise; just avoid hypotension. After thrombectomy, observational data favor systolic below 160. Without thrombolysis, treat pressure only above 220 over 120 unless there is end organ damage. Continue home beta blockers at reduced dose and clonidine at half dose to avoid rebound.

Malignant edema is most likely in young patients with carotid or M1 occlusion, poor collaterals, and early infarct signs in more than two thirds of the hemisphere. The most specific clinical sign is interval decline in consciousness. Intracranial pressure is not globally elevated early; deterioration comes from midline displacement. Hemicraniectomy within 48 hours, better within 24, without waiting for herniation, and dominant hemisphere is not a reason to withhold. Under 60, pooled trials showed mortality fell from 71 to 22 percent and independence rose from 2 to 14 percent. DESTINY 2 in patients aged 60 to 80 cut mortality from 76 to 43 percent, but no patient achieved independence. Cerebellar infarction with mass effect needs suboccipital decompression, and a ventriculostomy alone is not enough. Steroids do not work for cytotoxic edema. Edaravone is the only neuroprotectant ever positive. Secondary prevention: dual antiplatelet for 90 days in intracranial atherosclerosis, endarterectomy over stenting for carotid stenosis above 50 percent especially over age 70, direct oral anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation, warfarin with a ratio of 2.5 to 3.5 for mechanical valves, and foramen ovale closure under age 60. Fever within 24 hours is from the stroke; later fever is infection.

## Intracerebral hemorrhage

Hypertension causes 60 to 70 percent of hemorrhages via lipohyalinosis, in the basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebellum, and pons. Amyloid angiopathy causes lobar hemorrhage in the elderly, at the gray white junction, with tripled recurrence in apolipoprotein E epsilon 2 and epsilon 4 carriers. Volume is A times B times C divided by 2, and divided by 3 for irregular anticoagulant clots. The I C H score: 2 points for Glasgow 3 to 4, 1 point for Glasgow 5 to 12, 1 for volume 30 milliliters or more, 1 for intraventricular blood, 1 for infratentorial origin, 1 for age 80 or older. Expansion of 33 percent or more occurs in about 38 percent of patients scanned within 3 hours. The spot sign has 63 percent sensitivity and 90 percent specificity for expansion. Angiography is indicated under age 45, atypical location, no hypertension, or primary intraventricular hemorrhage.

Blood pressure. INTERACT 2 found no difference in the primary endpoint with a target of 140 versus 180 but a favorable ordinal analysis. ATACH 2 stopped for futility with 2.3 fold more renal adverse events in the intensive arm. Guidelines say rapid reduction to 140 is probably safe when presenting systolic is 150 to 220. Use labetalol, nicardipine, or clevidipine. Avoid nitroprusside and hydralazine.

Reversal. For warfarin with a ratio of 1.4 or higher, four factor prothrombin complex concentrate plus 10 milligrams of intravenous vitamin K; fresh frozen plasma at 10 to 15 milliliters per kilogram only if concentrate is unavailable. INCH showed faster normalization and less expansion with concentrate. Protamine: 1 milligram per 100 units of heparin in the last 4 hours, maximum 50 milligrams; for enoxaparin, 1 milligram per milligram if within 8 hours, half that if later. Dabigatran: idarucizumab 5 grams as two 2.5 gram doses, charcoal if within 2 hours. Factor Xa inhibitors: andexanet, which reduced anti Xa activity by 89 to 93 percent with rebound at 2 hours and a 10 percent thrombotic event rate, or four factor concentrate at 37.5 to 50 units per kilogram. The PATCH trial showed platelet transfusion for antiplatelet associated hemorrhage increased death and dependence, so do not transfuse. TICH 2 tranexamic acid and recombinant factor seven both reduced expansion without improving outcome and are not recommended.

Surgery. STICH and STICH 2 showed early surgery neither helps nor harms supratentorial hemorrhage. Cerebellar hemorrhage above 3 centimeters, or with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus, gets evacuation, and a drain alone is specifically discouraged. MISTIE 3 reached its 15 milliliter residual goal and improved mortality but not function; CLEAR 3 intraventricular alteplase likewise. Seizure prophylaxis is recommended against. Intermittent pneumatic compression from day 1 per CLOTS 3, no compression stockings, and subcutaneous heparin from day 2 once the clot is stable. Defer new do not resuscitate orders until the second full hospital day. Long term pressure goal is 130 over 80. Restart anticoagulation after about 4 weeks, at 7 to 10 days only for mechanical valves, and never in lobar amyloid hemorrhage.

## Subarachnoid hemorrhage

Eighty five to 90 percent of aneurysms are anterior circulation. Third nerve palsy means posterior communicating artery. Bilateral leg weakness and abulia mean anterior communicating. Terson syndrome is vitreous hemorrhage from raised pressure. Computed tomography sensitivity is 98 to 100 percent at 12 hours, 90 to 95 at 24 hours, 80 percent at 3 days, and 50 percent at 7 days. Lumbar puncture shows xanthochromia, which takes 6 to 12 hours to appear and lasts about 2 weeks. Catheter angiography is the gold standard; if negative with a non perimesencephalic pattern, repeat at 7 to 14 days, because up to 24 percent reveal an aneurysm. Perimesencephalic hemorrhage is 10 percent of cases and 60 percent of angiogram negative cases, is presumed venous, and needs no repeat study.

Modified Fisher: grade 4 is thick blood plus intraventricular hemorrhage with a 40 percent risk of delayed ischemia and 28 percent infarction; grade 3 is thick without ventricular blood, 19 and 12; grade 2 is thin with ventricular blood, 21 and 14; grade 1 is thin alone, 12 and 6. Hunt and Hess is subjective; the World Federation scale uses Glasgow score and motor deficit.

Rebleeding is 4 to 15 percent in the first 24 hours, highest in the first 6 hours, with mortality up to 70 percent. Before securing, keep systolic below 160 and mean below 110 using nicardipine, clevidipine, or labetalol infusion, avoiding hydralazine. Secure within 72 hours. If repair is delayed, tranexamic acid 1 gram then 1 gram every 6 hours for a maximum of 72 hours, never starting after 48 hours. ISAT: coiling gave 23.7 percent dead or dependent versus 30.6 for clipping. Clip middle cerebral aneurysms, wide necks, and large hematomas; coil the elderly, poor grade, and basilar apex.

Hydrocephalus occurs in 15 to 20 percent. Vasospasm begins day 3 to 5, spans days 4 to 14, and peaks days 5 to 7; 70 percent narrow but only 20 to 30 percent develop deficits. Delayed cerebral ischemia is focal worsening or a 2 point Glasgow fall for at least 1 hour not explained by other causes. Nimodipine 60 milligrams every 4 hours for 21 days, reduced to 30 every 2 hours if hypotensive, improves outcome without reversing angiographic spasm. Magnesium, statins, and clazosentan are all negative. Transcranial Doppler: mean velocity above 120 with a Lindegaard ratio above 3 is worrisome; above 200 with a ratio above 6 is severe; a ratio below 3 means hyperemia. Treat with euvolemia and induced hypertension, mean pressure 20 above baseline, systolic 160 to 220. Triple H is dead; prophylactic hypervolemia is harmful. Then angioplasty or intra arterial nicardipine, milrinone, or verapamil. Avoid phenytoin prophylaxis; if used, levetiracetam for 3 to 7 days. Hyponatremia in 30 percent, never fluid restrict. Fever in 70 to 80 percent, keep below 38.3. Hemoglobin 8 to 10. Heparin prophylaxis 24 hours after obliteration. Transfer to centers seeing more than 35 cases per year. Screen for aneurysms with two or more affected relatives or polycystic kidney disease.

## Traumatic brain injury

Severity: Glasgow 3 to 8 severe, 9 to 12 moderate, 13 to 15 mild. Epidural hematoma is lens shaped, does not cross sutures, and shows a lucid interval in about 47 percent. Subdural is crescent shaped from bridging veins and crosses sutures. Diffuse axonal injury gives depressed arousal out of proportion to the scan. A single systolic pressure below 90 doubles mortality. The Brain Trauma Foundation target is systolic at or above 100 for ages 50 to 69 and at or above 110 for ages 15 to 49 or over 70. Resuscitate with isotonic crystalloid; albumin may harm. CRASH 3 tranexamic acid missed its primary endpoint but reduced head injury death in mild to moderate injury.

Surgical thresholds. Subdural thicker than 10 millimeters or shift above 5 millimeters regardless of Glasgow score; a thinner subdural in a comatose patient if the score fell more than 2 points, pupils are abnormal, or pressure exceeds 20. Epidural above 30 cubic centimeters regardless of score. Contusions above 20 cubic centimeters with 5 millimeters of shift in patients scoring 6 to 8, or any lesion above 50.

BEST TRIP showed monitor guided therapy was not superior to clinical and imaging guided therapy. The ventricular drain is the gold standard monitor because it does not drift and can drain. Hourly documentation misses a quarter of elevations. The Seattle consensus lists what to avoid: continuous mannitol, scheduled hyperosmolar dosing, lumbar drainage, furosemide, routine steroids, routine cooling below 35, high dose propofol burst suppression, routine carbon dioxide below 30, and routine perfusion pressure above 90. Hypothermia failed in NABIS H 1 and 2 and POLAR. DECRA early craniectomy lowered pressure but worsened function. RESCUEicp last tier craniectomy cut mortality from 49 to 27 percent, and at 12 months both mortality and function favored surgery. The MRC CRASH trial showed steroids increase mortality, the single high quality recommendation in the guidelines. Phenytoin for 7 days reduces early seizures without changing later epilepsy. Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity affects 8 to 13 percent, starts about 1 week out, and is treated with morphine and propranolol; metoprolol does not work. Heparin prophylaxis within 24 to 48 hours, delayed to 72 hours for high risk lesions under the Parkland protocol. Jugular saturation above 50 percent and brain tissue oxygen above 15 are level three recommendations. Blast injury features vasospasm in up to half of cases for up to a month and edema within an hour.

## Spinal cord injury

Neurogenic shock is bradycardia plus hypotension from lesions at or above T4. Target a mean arterial pressure of 85 to 90 for 7 days on class three evidence. Treat with euvolemia and an alpha agonist, adding norepinephrine for beta effect, avoiding dopamine. Atropine 0.3 milligrams for reflex bradycardia with suctioning. Midodrine and pseudoephedrine help wean pressors; fludrocortisone does not. The phrenic nerve is C3 to C5, so complete injuries above C5 need intubation; up to 80 percent of C3 to C5 injuries are eventually liberated. Emergent intubation is rapid sequence with in line stabilization; awake fiberoptic when there is time. Steroids are contraindicated, and every major society recommends against them. Early surgery within 24 hours nearly triples the odds of a 2 grade improvement. Nearly 90 percent of grade A patients do not regain function, 54 percent of grade B regain motor function, and 86 percent of motor incomplete patients walk. Prophylaxis within 72 hours. Autonomic dysreflexia occurs above T6 with bladder or bowel triggers in 48 to 90 percent; sit the patient up and remove the stimulus before any drug, then nicardipine or nitroprusside.

## Seizures and status epilepticus

Convulsive status epilepticus: time point 1 is 5 minutes, time point 2 is 30 minutes. Refractory means failure of first and second line drugs; super refractory means continuing after 24 hours of anesthetics. About one third of intensive care status is refractory, and one third of refractory patients die. After convulsions stop with benzodiazepines, 48 percent have ongoing nonconvulsive seizures and 14 percent are in nonconvulsive status. If a patient is not waking 15 to 20 minutes after convulsions stop, assume nonconvulsive status and get continuous electroencephalography. Treated within 30 minutes, 80 percent respond to the first drug; after 2 hours, only 40 percent. Benzodiazepine tachyphylaxis comes from receptor internalization. Cefepime causes seizures by gabba antagonism.

First line: lorazepam 0.1 milligrams per kilogram to a maximum of 4 per dose at 2 milligrams per minute, repeatable in 5 to 10 minutes; its duration against status is 4 to 14 hours versus 20 minutes for diazepam. The Veterans Affairs study: lorazepam 65 percent, phenobarbital 58, diazepam plus phenytoin 56, phenytoin alone 44. Without access, midazolam 0.2 milligrams per kilogram intramuscularly to 10 milligrams per RAMPART. Second line per ESETT: valproate 40 milligrams per kilogram, fosphenytoin 20 per kilogram, levetiracetam 60 per kilogram to 4500 maximum, all about 45 to 47 percent effective. Fosphenytoin at 50 rather than 150 per minute to avoid hypotension. Avoid valproate with carbapenems and in pregnancy. Lacosamide 200 to 600 milligrams prolongs the PR interval. Refractory: midazolam or propofol infusions, only in intubated patients, titrated to seizure cessation or burst suppression for 24 to 48 hours, then wean propofol, ketamine, and pentobarbital by 20 percent every 3 hours and midazolam by 50 percent every 3 hours. Ketamine 2 to 5 milligrams per kilogram load, 1.5 to 10 per hour, and it does not cause hypotension. Pentobarbital 5 milligrams per kilogram load, 0.5 to 10 per hour, self tapers with a half life of 15 to 50 hours. HYBERNATUS hypothermia improved electrographic control but not function. The two HELPS two B score gives 2 points to brief ictal rhythmic discharges. Myoclonic status after arrest is not automatically hopeless: burst suppression with polyspikes locked to jerks predicted death in 100 percent, but a continuous background allowed 50 percent independence. Prophylaxis: not in intracerebral hemorrhage or ischemic stroke, may consider briefly after aneurysmal hemorrhage, 7 days after moderate to severe head injury.

## Neuromuscular disorders

The 20 30 40 rule: vital capacity below 20 milliliters per kilogram, inspiratory pressure weaker than minus 30, expiratory pressure below 40, or a 30 percent fall in any, predicts respiratory failure. Paradoxical breathing means diaphragm failure and immediate intubation. Counting to 20 on one breath approximates a preserved vital capacity. Hypercarbia is a late finding, so a normal gas never excludes neuromuscular failure; hypoxia is also late.

Guillain Barre: albuminocytologic dissociation that may take days to appear; intravenous immunoglobulin can cause aseptic meningitis and confound it. Nerve conduction is most abnormal at 2 weeks; sural sparing with abnormal median sensory conduction is classic. Acute motor axonal neuropathy follows campylobacter with GM1 antibodies; Miller Fisher has GQ1b with ataxia, areflexia, and ophthalmoplegia. Progression beyond 4 weeks suggests chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. Plasma exchange is 5 exchanges over 1 to 2 weeks. Immunoglobulin is 2 grams per kilogram total, over 5 or 2 days, equivalent to exchange with fewer adverse events. Steroids alone do nothing. Bilevel positive airway pressure is not safe in Guillain Barre; intubate for vital capacity under 15 to 20 or any hypercarbia, then rest on full support for 24 to 48 hours. Dysautonomia: avoid neostigmine and metoclopramide for ileus, consider a pacemaker after symptomatic bradyarrhythmia, treat only sustained severe hypertension with short acting agents, and use esmolol if a beta blocker is truly needed.

Myasthenic crisis is class 5, ventilatory failure, usually within the first 8 to 12 months, precipitated by infection in a third to a half. Unlike Guillain Barre, early bilevel positive airway pressure while carbon dioxide is still normal can avert intubation and halve ventilation time. There are no validated pulmonary function cutoffs because facial weakness spoils the seal. Restart pyridostigmine before weaning, use long breathing trials, and give nocturnal bilevel support after extubation. Repetitive stimulation shows a decrement of 10 percent or more; single fiber electromyography is 95 percent sensitive; the ice pack test improves ptosis. Acetylcholine receptor antibodies in up to 85 percent, muscle specific kinase antibodies in half of the seronegative with bulbar predominance and rituximab response, thymoma in up to 11 percent, striational antibodies in 90 percent of thymoma, and every patient needs a chest scan. Steroids at 1 milligram per kilogram are standard, but high dose methylprednisolone worsens weakness within days in a third to a half of nonintubated patients. Lambert Eaton is presynaptic calcium channel, proximal legs first, paraneoplastic in 50 to 60 percent with small cell cancer. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, tracheostomy adds about 10 months, and cough assist is indicated when peak cough flow falls below 270 liters per minute. Critical illness neuromyopathy affects 60 to 80 percent, creatine kinase is usually normal, and direct muscle stimulation showing inexcitable muscle points to myopathy.

## Coma, brain death, and prognostication

Preserved pupillary reflexes with depressed respiration or motor findings suggest metabolic coma. Pinpoint pupils mean pontine lesion or opioids; midposition fixed means midbrain or brain death. Locked in syndrome is ventral pontine, communicating by blinking and vertical eye movement. Cold caloric testing is done at 30 degrees head elevation; an intact brainstem gives slow tonic deviation toward the cold ear. The FOUR score adds brainstem reflexes and respiration. Coma of 10 days or more becomes unresponsive wakefulness, permanent after 3 months for nontraumatic and 12 months for traumatic injury. Unresponsive wakefulness syndrome is the modern name for the vegetative state. Empiric meningitis coverage is ceftriaxone 2 grams every 12 hours, vancomycin 20 milligrams per kilogram every 12 hours, acyclovir 10 per kilogram every 8 hours, and ampicillin, given before imaging or spinal fluid.

Brain death prerequisites: known irreversible cause, core temperature at or above 36 degrees, no hypotension, no intoxication, no neuromuscular blockade by train of four, no metabolic derangement, and 5 half lives after sedatives with normal hepatic and renal function. The cardinal findings are coma, brainstem areflexia, and apnea. Decerebrate or decorticate posturing excludes brain death; spinal reflexes like triple flexion, Babinski, and the Lazarus sign are permitted. The apnea test starts from a carbon dioxide of 40 to 45 after preoxygenation, watched for 8 to 10 minutes on 4 to 6 liters of oxygen, and is positive when carbon dioxide rises above 60 or by 20 in a retainer. Ancillary tests are not required and are reserved for when the exam or apnea test cannot be completed. Time of death is when the confirmatory gas is reported or the ancillary test is read. Anticipate diabetes insipidus, desmopressin, and volume to a central pressure of 8 to 12.

Prognostication principles: neurointensivists predict poor outcome with 94 percent accuracy but good outcome with only 63 percent. Discrimination is separating those with and without an outcome; calibration is whether predicted probability matches observed. Early do not resuscitate orders reduce aggressive care and increase death independent of severity. Cohort data must not predict an individual. At least 30 percent of severe head injury patients die in hospital, 70 percent of those after withdrawal, most within 3 days when prognosis is least certain. The Coma Recovery Scale Revised reduces misdiagnosis of consciousness. Bilaterally absent N20 potentials predict poor outcome after arrest.

## Cardiac arrest and temperature management

Two thirds of deaths after successful resuscitation are neurologic, and 80 percent of those follow withdrawal for perceived poor prognosis. PARAMEDIC 2: epinephrine improved survival with a number needed to treat of 112 without improving neurologic outcome. Bag mask ventilation equaled intubation prehospital. Amiodarone and lidocaine do not improve survival. COACT: delaying angiography without ST elevation did not affect survival, though 25 to 58 percent still have a culprit lesion. Every patient with a Glasgow motor score below 6 after return of circulation is a candidate for temperature management. Targets: 32 to 37.5 degrees for 24 hours, rewarming no faster than a quarter to a half degree per hour, fever prevention for 72 hours, mean pressure above 65, saturation above 94 percent. Hypothermia After Cardiac Arrest: 32 to 34 degrees gave favorable outcome in 55 versus 39 percent, number needed to treat 6. T T M 1: 33 versus 36, no difference. T T M 2 in 1861 patients: 33 versus normothermia, identical outcomes, more arrhythmia with cooling at 24 versus 16 percent. HYPERION supports 33 degrees for nonshockable rhythms, favorable outcome 10.2 versus 5.7 percent. Prehospital cold saline increased rearrest and pulmonary edema. Twenty four and 48 hours of cooling were equivalent. Hypothermia lowers metabolic rate 8 percent per degree, causes cold diuresis and intracellular potassium shift at 34 to 35 degrees, and below 32 prolongs PR and QT. If the device water is cold and flowing but the patient stays warm, the patient is shivering; refractory shivering on high dose sedation gets paralysis. Buspirone is the only oral antishivering drug; meperidine is most effective but lowers the seizure threshold, with 12.5 milligrams as good as 100. Seizures occur in 15 to 33 percent and nonconvulsive status in 12 to 32 percent; no prophylaxis. Prognosticate at 72 hours after normothermia off sedation. Somatosensory potentials at 24 hours if not cooled and 72 hours if cooled. A motor score of 2 or less has a 10 to 35 percent false positive rate. Neuron specific enolase of 33 at 48 hours is 65 percent sensitive and 91 percent specific. Always multimodal.

## Neuroinfectious disease

The meningitis triad is present in only 44 percent, but two of headache, fever, stiff neck, and altered mentation reach 95 percent sensitivity. Image before puncture for depressed consciousness, focal signs, or immunocompromise, and start antibiotics without waiting. Bacterial fluid: high pressure, high protein, neutrophils, glucose ratio below 0.6. Empiric therapy is vancomycin plus a third generation cephalosporin, adding ampicillin over age 50 or with immunocompromise; never rifampin alone. Dexamethasone 0.15 milligrams per kilogram every 6 hours for 2 to 4 days for pneumococcus, given 10 to 20 minutes before or with the first antibiotic. Durations: 7 days for meningococcus and haemophilus, 10 to 14 for pneumococcus, 21 for listeria and pseudomonas. Basilar skull fracture points to pneumococcus and haemophilus; neurosurgery to staph and pseudomonas; shunts to coagulase negative staph and propionibacterium. Cerebrospinal fluid lactate at or above 4 in a postoperative patient should trigger empiric therapy; above 4.2 was 96 percent sensitive and 100 percent specific. Protocolized drain care halved ventriculitis from 9.9 to 4.6 percent; impregnated catheters help, prolonged prophylactic antibiotics and routine drain exchange do not. Ventriculitis treatment is vancomycin plus cefepime, 10 to 14 days, 21 for gram negatives, and remove the hardware.

All suspected encephalitis gets acyclovir 10 milligrams per kilogram of ideal weight every 8 hours over 1 hour with hydration until the herpes polymerase chain reaction is negative; confirmed disease is treated 14 to 21 days. Herpes 1 is the most common fatal encephalitis; temporal periodic discharges are 60 percent sensitive and 80 percent specific. Herpes 2 causes recurrent Mollaret meningitis and recurrences are not treated. West Nile causes flaccid paralysis and parkinsonism with basal ganglia and thalamic lesions; diagnose by cerebrospinal fluid immunoglobulin M, since polymerase chain reaction is under 60 percent sensitive. Cytomegalovirus gets ganciclovir plus foscarnet. Brain abscess above 2.5 centimeters needs surgery; empiric therapy is vancomycin, a cephalosporin, and metronidazole for 6 to 8 weeks. Subdural empyema shows restricted diffusion, needs drainage in 96 percent, and lumbar puncture is contraindicated. Cryptococcus gets amphotericin with flucytosine for induction, then a 12 to 18 month course. Drug induced aseptic meningitis classically comes from amoxicillin, nonsteroidals, and trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole.

## Neuroimmunology, encephalopathy, and delirium

Antibodies to intracellular antigens, Hu, Ri, Yo, and CRMP5, mark T cell mediated irreversible disease. Antibodies to surface antigens, N methyl D aspartate receptor, LGI1, CASPR2, aquaporin 4, MOG, are reversible with fast immunotherapy. A recognizable paraneoplastic syndrome with a visible tumor means resect now without waiting for serology. Extreme delta brush means anti N methyl D aspartate receptor encephalitis, which causes seizures in over 80 percent acutely and is linked to ovarian teratoma. Inflammatory fluid is more than 5 white cells, protein above 50, more than one oligoclonal band, or an immunoglobulin G index above 0.66, and normal cells and protein do not exclude autoimmune encephalitis. Low glucose among inflammatory conditions suggests neurosarcoidosis, primary angiitis, or Behcet. Negative body imaging leads to whole body positron emission tomography. Induction doses: methylprednisolone 1 gram for 3 to 5 days, immunoglobulin 2 grams per kilogram, exchange of 1 to 1.5 volumes for 5 sessions, rituximab 1000 milligrams twice 2 weeks apart. In chimeric antigen receptor T cell neurotoxicity, tocilizumab does not work and may worsen it; use steroids, then siltuximab or anakinra, and antithymocyte globulin for cerebral edema. Checkpoint inhibitors classically cause hypophysitis, myositis, and myasthenia. A JC virus index above 1.5 raises progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy risk.

Delirium affects up to 80 percent of intensive care patients, is hypoactive in 44 percent, mixed in 54 percent, and hyperactive in only about 2 percent; hypoactive delirium in the elderly has the worst prognosis. Screening tools confirm delirium but never identify the cause. Haloperidol 1 to 5 milligrams every 4 to 6 hours with QT and magnesium monitoring is no better than atypicals. Benzodiazepines only for alcohol or cocaine withdrawal. Dexmedetomidine produced less delirium than midazolam or lorazepam. Lactulose is first line for hepatic encephalopathy with rifaximin 550 twice daily added for prophylaxis, and intracranial pressure monitoring in acute liver failure did not improve mortality. Triphasic waves appear in hepatic and uremic encephalopathy; the lentiform fork sign is uremic. Wernicke gets thiamine 200 to 500 milligrams every 8 hours; eye signs improve in hours to days, ataxia often does not. Posterior reversible encephalopathy and hypertensive encephalopathy are managed by controlling pressure.

## Multimodality neuromonitoring

Indications: acute brain injury, an examination limited by arousal that will not improve within 24 hours, and high risk of deterioration. Relative contraindications include age over 80, international normalized ratio above 1.5, direct oral anticoagulants, dual antiplatelets, and expected death within 48 hours. Bolt hemorrhage is about 2 percent and infection 0.6 to 2.1 percent, versus 6 to 7 percent hemorrhage and about 8 percent infection for a ventricular drain. Always scan after placement. Pressure reactivity index is the moving correlation of mean arterial and intracranial pressure; values above 0.25 to 0.3 mean impaired autoregulation. Plotting it against perfusion pressure gives a U shaped curve whose nadir is optimal perfusion pressure, about 70 after trauma and 78 to 98 in high grade subarachnoid hemorrhage with ischemia. If raising blood pressure lowers intracranial pressure, autoregulation is intact and you are below the lower limit, so raise pressure rather than escalate osmotherapy. Normal brain tissue oxygen is 25 to 35; below 20 is the treatment threshold and below 15 is ischemia. A failed oxygen challenge means a bad probe. BOOST 2 reduced hypoxia from 44 to 15 percent with a trend to better outcome. Jugular saturation below 50 percent is the treatment threshold and is the only oxygenation monitor with Brain Trauma Foundation level three support. About 90 percent of intensive care seizures are found only on continuous electroencephalography; nonconvulsive seizures occur in 8 to 48 percent of critically ill patients. A scalp visible seizure needs 6 to 10 centimeters of cortex, so depth electrodes find 40 percent more. Quantitative electroencephalography flags delayed ischemia 2 days early. Spreading depolarizations travel at 3 millimeters per minute and need a full band amplifier. Lactate to pyruvate ratio with pyruvate below 70 means ischemia; with pyruvate above 70 it means mitochondrial dysfunction, which is 5 times more common after trauma. Brain glucose below 0.8 millimoles is low, and tight serum glucose below 120 starves the brain.

## Sedation, analgesia, and pharmacology

Light sedation, Richmond scale 0 to minus 2, is the default; early deep sedation predicts mortality out to 2 years. Analgesia first, then propofol or dexmedetomidine rather than benzodiazepines, per the 2018 guidelines. Deep sedation at minus 4 to minus 5 is required before neuromuscular blockade, which is titrated to 1 to 2 twitches on train of four; 4 twitches means less than 75 percent block and 1 twitch means 90 percent. Cisatracurium is preferred in organ failure because of Hofmann degradation; atracurium is avoided because laudanosine causes seizures. Wake up tests are risky in the injured brain: 30 percent aborted, 70 percent of aborted trials had brain hypoxia, and only 2 percent found a new deficit.

Propofol only in intubated patients. Propofol infusion syndrome: heart failure, bradycardia, lactic acidosis, hyperlipidemia, hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis, with risk above 75 to 80 micrograms per kilogram per minute for more than 48 hours, low body mass, youth, and pressors or steroids; mortality up to 80 percent; treat by stopping the drug, pacing, and dialysis. Rising creatine kinase and triglycerides is the presyndrome. Green urine is benign. Dexmedetomidine is an alpha 2 agonist that spares respiration, dosed 0.1 to 1.5 micrograms per kilogram per hour starting at 0.2, no boluses, and it does not prevent alcohol withdrawal seizures because it has no gabba activity. Midazolam is the benzodiazepine for refractory status because lorazepam and diazepam infusions carry propylene glycol toxicity, which presents as lactic acidosis, hyperosmolality, and renal failure; an osmolar gap above 12 signals accumulation. Alpha hydroxymidazolam accumulates in renal failure. Ketamine preserves respiratory drive, does not raise intracranial pressure by meta analysis, and can cause mydriasis. Fentanyl is 600 times more lipid soluble than morphine, has no active metabolite, is preferred in hemodynamic instability, and rarely causes chest wall rigidity. Avoid morphine in renal failure because the 3 glucuronide is neurotoxic. Never use meperidine for pain because normeperidine causes seizures. Acetaminophen at or below 4 grams per day.

Pharmacokinetics. Augmented renal clearance is a filtration rate above 130, requiring levetiracetam 1000 milligrams every 8 hours and meropenem 2 grams every 8 hours. Highly protein bound drugs, phenytoin, valproate, warfarin, diazepam, need free levels; albumin correction equations are unreliable. Phenytoin total 10 to 20, free 1 to 2, nonlinear kinetics, no more than 400 milligrams at a time. Valproate 50 to 100, carbamazepine 4 to 12, phenobarbital 15 to 40. Vasopressin 0.03 units per minute in vasodilatory shock, 20 to 30 milliunits per hour for diabetes insipidus. Norepinephrine and dopamine are preferred with coexisting bradycardia.

## Pregnancy, pediatrics, perioperative, and rehabilitation

Pregnancy raises blood volume 45 to 50 percent and cardiac output 30 to 50 percent, while blood pressure barely changes. Baseline carbon dioxide is 27 to 34, functional residual capacity falls 20 percent, so desaturation is fast. Eclampsia is preeclampsia plus seizure or coma, up to 48 hours postpartum, treated with magnesium and delivered, with pressure controlled by labetalol, nifedipine, or hydralazine; angiotensin agents are teratogenic. Stroke risk is 30 per 100,000 deliveries, peaking in the third trimester and peripartum. Alteplase does not cross the placenta and thrombectomy is safe, so treat as nonpregnant. Cerebral venous thrombosis is the most common neurovascular complication, mostly in the first 6 postpartum weeks, treated with heparin; warfarin is contraindicated. Levetiracetam is the safest antiseizure drug in pregnancy. Listeria is the one pathogen increased by pregnancy; ampicillin plus gentamicin. In maternal arrest, displace the uterus manually leftward, access above the diaphragm, standard drug doses, and perimortem cesarean within 4 minutes.

Pediatrics. Up to 90 percent of children with acute focal deficits have a mimic. Alteplase may be considered from age 2 to 17 with proven infarct and occlusion and a pediatric stroke scale of 6 to 24, never under 2. Aspirin 2 to 5 milligrams per kilogram, avoiding it under 12 with chicken pox or influenza. Hypothermia after pediatric arrest did not help, so target 36 to 37.5 for 48 hours. Epinephrine 0.01 milligrams per kilogram every 3 to 5 minutes. Pediatric intracranial pressure goal below 20 with perfusion pressure of 45 to 55 under age 6 and 50 to 60 above; 3 percent saline 2 to 5 milliliters per kilogram, hold for sodium 160 or osmolality 360; hyperventilation only under 10 minutes for herniation. Seizure prophylaxis for 7 days. ESETT applies to children equally. Propofol is contraindicated for pediatric seizures. Anakinra for febrile infection related epilepsy syndrome. Dexamethasone 0.15 milligrams per kilogram every 6 hours for 4 days in bacterial meningitis; acyclovir 20 milligrams per kilogram in neonates.

Perioperative. Seizures after craniotomy occur in 15 to 20 percent and up to 25 percent after subdural evacuation; prophylaxis mostly does not help and phenytoin was linked to harm. Edema peaks at 48 to 72 hours. Large hemorrhage complicates 1 in 300 craniotomies. After posterior fossa surgery, refractory hypertension means hematoma until proven otherwise; image, do not just treat the pressure. Seated position risks venous air embolism in 20 percent. Beta 2 transferrin confirms a cerebrospinal fluid leak. Most postoperative polyuria is not diabetes insipidus. An expanding neck hematoma after carotid or anterior cervical surgery is opened at the bedside before intubation. Carotid endarterectomy causes cranial nerve deficits in 5 percent. Ondansetron prolongs the QT.

Rehabilitation. AVERT showed very early mobilization within 24 hours worsened outcome, especially in severe stroke and hemorrhage, while mobilization at 48 hours beat 7 days in intracerebral hemorrhage. Bedside swallow screening within 24 hours; videofluoroscopy is the gold standard. Amantadine has the best evidence for arousal after severe head injury. Posttraumatic agitation resolves in 7 to 10 days, first line propranolol; avoid benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, and typical antipsychotics that delay recovery. One third of stroke survivors have depression; FOCUS showed fluoxetine does not improve motor recovery. Complete the spinal injury classification exam within 24 hours.

## Administration and quality

High intensity intensivist staffing improves survival and shortens stay. Closed units are intensivist run, open units are directed by anyone, semi closed are comanaged. Staffing is 46 to 56 percent of the budget. Triage criteria must be written in advance and may be applied without consent. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act requires a screening exam for anyone on the campus within 250 yards, and does not apply to admitted inpatients; an unstabilized transfer requires certification that benefits outweigh risks, and the receiving hospital must accept if it has capacity. Estimate 8 to 12 neurocritical care beds per 400 to 500 hospital beds. Value is outcomes divided by cost. Donabedian measures are structure, process, and outcome; a drain bundle is structure, giving nimodipine is process, the 6 month Rankin is outcome, and a metric that detects unintended harm, like readmissions when you shorten stay, is a balance measure. The mortality index is observed over expected, and undercoding comorbidities makes a hospital look worse. The Neurocritical Care Society published unit standards in 2018 and 21 performance measures in 2020.
